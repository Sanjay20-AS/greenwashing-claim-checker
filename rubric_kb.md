# GreenClaim Verification Knowledge Base

## Purpose
This knowledge base provides a lightweight rubric for evaluating environmental and sustainability claims in corporate reports.

The system does not determine whether a company is guilty of greenwashing, fraud, or deception.

It checks whether a claim contains sufficient information and evidence within the supplied document.

## 1. Claim Specificity
A specific environmental claim should identify what environmental improvement is being claimed.

Examples of relatively specific claims:
- "We reduced Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions by 18% from our 2020 baseline."
- "Our packaging contains 60% post-consumer recycled plastic."
- "We aim to reduce absolute Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 50% by 2030."

Examples of vague claims:
- "We are committed to a greener future."
- "Our products are eco-friendly."
- "We are making our operations more sustainable."
- "Our packaging is better for the planet."

Vague environmental language should be treated as requiring additional evidence.

## 2. Measurability
Strong quantitative claims normally provide measurable information.

Useful elements include:
- numerical result
- baseline year
- target year
- percentage reduction
- absolute quantity
- measurement period
- clearly defined metric

Example:
"We reduced operational emissions by 25% compared with our 2020 baseline."

A statement such as "We significantly reduced our carbon footprint" does not explain the baseline period and therefore needs additional evidence.

## 3. Evidence
Environmental claims are stronger when accompanied by evidence.

Evidence can include:
- quantitative measurements
- methodology
- reporting boundaries
- assurance statements
- third-party verification
- audit information
- recognized standards
- references to GHG Protocol
- references to Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi)
- references to ISO 14001
- documented measurement procedures

The absence of such information does not prove that a claim is false. It means that the supplied document may not contain enough evidence for verification.

## 4. Baselines
A percentage improvement should ideally identify the reference point.

Example:
"We reduced Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 30% from our 2020 baseline."

## 5. Target Dates
Forward-looking claims should ideally provide a timeline.

Example:
"We aim to achieve a 50% reduction in Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 2030."

A target year alone does not demonstrate that a target is scientifically validated or achievable.

## 6. Scope Clarity
Environmental claims should make their scope clear.

Useful scope information includes:
- company-wide vs product-specific
- geographic boundary
- operational boundary
- Scope 1
- Scope 2
- Scope 3
- specific facility
- specific product line

## 7. GHG Protocol
The Greenhouse Gas Protocol provides widely used frameworks for accounting and reporting greenhouse gas emissions.

Scope 1: Direct greenhouse gas emissions from sources owned or controlled by the organization.
Scope 2: Indirect emissions associated with purchased or acquired electricity, steam, heating, and cooling.
Scope 3: Other indirect value-chain emissions.

Mentioning a recognized accounting methodology can make a claim easier to interpret and evaluate. It is not by itself proof that every claim is accurate.

## 8. Science Based Targets Initiative (SBTi)
Science-based climate targets are intended to align corporate greenhouse gas reduction pathways with climate science.

For this prototype, an SBTi reference is treated as a positive evidence signal. The system should not independently certify whether a target is actually SBTi-approved unless authoritative verification data is available.

## 9. ISO 14001
ISO 14001 is an environmental management system standard.

Mentioning ISO 14001 may provide context about an organization's environmental management system. Certification of an environmental management system does not automatically prove every environmental marketing claim.

## 10. Common Greenwashing Risk Patterns
### Vague language
Examples:
- green
- eco-friendly
- environmentally friendly
- planet-friendly
- sustainable
- clean
- natural
- responsible
- better for the environment

These words can be legitimate when supported by specific evidence, but should be reviewed when used without measurable details.

### Hidden trade-offs
A claim may emphasize one environmental benefit while leaving out relevant environmental impacts.

### No proof
A claim may describe a significant environmental benefit without providing data, methodology, measurement period, baseline, verification, or supporting documentation.

### Irrelevant claims
An environmental statement may be technically true but not meaningful to the environmental impact of the product or company.

### Unclear labels
Terms such as green, carbon neutral, net zero, sustainable, climate positive, recyclable, biodegradable, and renewable should be accompanied by definitions, boundaries, methodology, or evidence where appropriate.

## 11. Status Rubric
### Well-supported
Use when the claim contains most of:
- clear environmental subject
- measurable information
- relevant baseline or measurement period
- timeline where appropriate
- clear scope
- supporting data or methodology
- credible standard/verification reference where relevant

### Needs more evidence
Use when the claim is reasonably specific but important supporting information is missing.

### Vague/Unsupported
Use when the statement makes a broad environmental claim but provides little or no supporting information.

"Vague/Unsupported" means insufficient evidence is provided in the document, not that the claim is false.

## 12. Transparency Score
Dimensions:
- Specificity: 25%
- Measurability: 25%
- Evidence: 30%
- Scope clarity: 20%

The score describes transparency/evidence completeness of claims in the supplied document. It is not a company ESG rating, legal compliance score, or proof that a company is or is not greenwashing.

## 13. Responsible AI
1. Avoid accusing organizations of fraud or deception.
2. Explain why a claim was flagged.
3. Show the original source quote.
4. Distinguish missing evidence from false information.
5. Encourage human review.
6. Avoid using personal or sensitive information.
7. Avoid treating the AI classification as legal or regulatory advice.
8. Preserve uncertainty when the document does not contain enough information.
